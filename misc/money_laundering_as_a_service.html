Money Laundering as a Service: Why the Collapsing Price is the Real Warning

Published on August 25, 2026

Money laundering has become a service business. And the price is collapsing. That's the part of the FATF warning that every compliance team should consider.

Giles Thomson, six weeks into his FATF presidency, highlighted that criminals are now selling laundering services at enormous scale for a fraction of what they used to charge.

What does a falling price actually mean? Prices fall when supply industrialises — when something that used to require relationships and personal risk becomes a product you can buy off the shelf. Mule networks recruited at scale; automated layering across dozens of institutions before anyone opens a case.

The criminal side has automated.

Most banks still investigate the way they did a decade ago: one analyst, seven systems, manually assembling a story that the other side generated in seconds.

That asymmetry is the whole problem. It isn't a headcount gap you can hire your way out of — the economics don't work, because their cost per attack is falling while your cost per investigation remains flat.

There's a second signal in the UK Presidency's published objectives that has been under-read. Alongside fraud, FATF wants countries and the private sector to focus on high-value reporting and actionable intelligence.

Read that as: nobody is going to be rewarded for raw volume anymore.

For mid-market banks, that's either a threat or the best news of the year. You're held to the same standard as a Tier 1 institution without Tier 1 headcount. A regime that values quality of intelligence over quantity of alerts is a regime where smaller teams can compete — but only if investigations are fast and well-evidenced enough to produce that quality.

Which is the real question for strategic planning: is your team's output measured in alerts cleared, or in intelligence produced?
