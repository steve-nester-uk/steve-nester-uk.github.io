Who Sees the Aggregate? How Jurisdictions are Answering AML Fragmentation

Published on November 4, 2026

In my post, “Structuring moved up a layer and payment AML controls haven’t followed it”, I asked who is supposed to see the aggregate. Around the world, answers are now forming.

A quick recap: if activity is spread across enough regulated entities, each one sees something normal. A mule network across five banks, or a merchant across five acquirers, keeps every institution inside its thresholds. Catching it means someone has to see the aggregate.

That’s the answer and the direction the industry is moving, though not at the same speed everywhere:

  • Singapore built it centrally. MAS launched COSMIC in April 2024 with six major banks. Sharing started out voluntary, and MAS has said some of it will become mandatory. This August, MAS told Parliament the platform has led to additional suspicious transaction reports and surfaced networks for review.
  • The UK built a legal gateway. Since January 2024, ECCTA has removed civil liability for regulated firms that share customer information to fight economic crime. That covers banks, EMIs, payment institutions and crypto firms. The door is open, but by the government’s own account, few firms have walked through it.
  • Germany has been piloting. safeAML, run through Hesse’s state data trustee with Commerzbank, Deutsche Bank and N26, tests cross-bank tracing using pseudonymised data.
  • The Netherlands went furthest and fastest. TMNL pooled transaction data from five banks for joint monitoring. It wound down in 2024 because it didn’t fit the incoming EU rules.

TMNL’s lesson is one the rest of Europe has taken note of. The barrier is rarely the technology. It’s data protection.

Which is why the most telling detail in the EU’s next step is who’s writing it. AMLA isn’t drafting the guidelines on Partnerships for Information Sharing alone. It’s drafting them jointly with the European Data Protection Board. Last week they invited technology providers with privacy-enhancing tech experience to a roundtable, with a public consultation expected in the first half of 2027.

The debate has shifted from whether to share to how: a central data pool, pseudonymised lookups, or computation that never exposes the underlying data.

One thing I’ll be watching: almost every scheme above is designed around bank accounts and mule chains. The payments side, where a merchant is spread across five acquirers and three orchestrated routes, is barely in the frame. Payment institutions are in scope in some regimes, but the use cases, data models and identity matching were built for retail banking.

If the aggregate is going to become visible, who else needs a seat at the table?
